Study Guide Overview

What you need to know — and why it matters in the real world

⚠️
What Is the Hazmat Endorsement? The CDL Hazmat Endorsement (HME) is required any time you transport hazardous materials in a quantity requiring placards OR any amount of a material listed as a Select Agent or Toxin. You must pass a written knowledge test AND a TSA security threat assessment (background check).
🔴
Federal Law — Not Optional Hazmat regulations come from two agencies: PHMSA (Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration) sets the standards for classifying, packaging, and communicating hazmat. FMCSA (Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration) governs hazmat transport by commercial motor vehicle. Both sets of rules apply to you.

Hazard Classes & Divisions

All 9 DOT hazard classes — memorize these cold

ℹ️
The 9 Hazard Classes Every hazardous material is assigned to one of 9 classes based on its primary danger. Some classes are divided into divisions (sub-groups). The class number appears on placards and shipping papers.
CLASS 1 — EXPLOSIVES

Explosives

Materials that can rapidly release energy through explosion, fire, or burst.

Divisions: 1.1 (Mass explosion) · 1.2 (Projection hazard) · 1.3 (Fire/minor blast) · 1.4 (Minor hazard) · 1.5 (Very insensitive) · 1.6 (Extremely insensitive)
Examples: Dynamite (1.1), Fireworks (1.3, 1.4), Ammunition (1.4)
Placard: Orange with explosion symbol. Division 1.4S may not require placard.
CLASS 2 — GASES

Gases

Materials that are gaseous at normal temperature and pressure.

Div 2.1: Flammable gas · Div 2.2: Non-flammable, non-toxic · Div 2.3: Poison/toxic gas
Examples: Propane (2.1), CO₂ (2.2), Chlorine (2.3)
Placard: 2.1 = Red, 2.2 = Green, 2.3 = White (POISON GAS)
CLASS 3 — FLAMMABLE LIQUIDS

Flammable & Combustible Liquids

Liquids with a flash point below 141°F (60.5°C). Combustible: 141°F–200°F.

No sub-divisions.
Examples: Gasoline, diesel, acetone, paint
Placard: Red diamond with flame symbol. "FLAMMABLE" or "COMBUSTIBLE"
CLASS 4 — FLAMMABLE SOLIDS

Flammable Solids / Spontaneous Combustion / Water-Reactive

Solids that burn easily or can ignite under specific conditions.

Div 4.1: Flammable solid · Div 4.2: Spontaneously combustible · Div 4.3: Dangerous when wet
Examples: Matches (4.1), White phosphorus (4.2), Sodium metal (4.3)
Placard: 4.1 = Red/white stripes, 4.2 = White/red, 4.3 = Blue
CLASS 5 — OXIDIZERS

Oxidizers & Organic Peroxides

Materials that yield oxygen easily and promote combustion of other materials.

Div 5.1: Oxidizer · Div 5.2: Organic peroxide
Examples: Hydrogen peroxide (5.1), Ammonium nitrate (5.1), Benzoyl peroxide (5.2)
Placard: Yellow with flame-over-circle symbol
CLASS 6 — POISONS

Toxic & Infectious Substances

Materials that are toxic to humans or carry infectious pathogens.

Div 6.1: Toxic substances · Div 6.2: Infectious substances
Examples: Pesticides (6.1), Medical waste (6.2)
Placard: 6.1 = White with skull; 6.2 = White with biohazard symbol
CLASS 7 — RADIOACTIVE

Radioactive Materials

Materials that spontaneously emit ionizing radiation.

No sub-divisions. Three label categories: I (white), II (yellow/white), III (yellow/white).
Examples: Medical isotopes, uranium ore, smoke detectors
Placard: Yellow/white with trefoil (radiation) symbol
CLASS 8 — CORROSIVES

Corrosives

Liquids or solids that destroy living tissue or severely corrode steel/aluminum.

No sub-divisions.
Examples: Battery acid (sulfuric acid), hydrochloric acid, sodium hydroxide (lye)
Placard: Black/white with liquid attacking metal/hand
CLASS 9 — MISCELLANEOUS

Miscellaneous Hazardous Materials

Hazardous materials that present a hazard during transport but don't fit other classes.

No sub-divisions.
Examples: Dry ice, lithium batteries, elevated temperature materials, magnetized materials
Placard: White with 7 vertical stripes (top) — only required for bulk quantities

⚡ Special Categories You Must Know

  • ORM-D (Other Regulated Materials – Domestic): Consumer commodities — treated less strictly. Still requires "ORM-D" marking.
  • Poison Inhalation Hazard (PIH): Extra-dangerous toxic gases/liquids that are dangerous when inhaled. Requires both POISON and INHALATION HAZARD placards. Examples: Anhydrous ammonia, chlorine, methyl bromide.
  • Marine Pollutants: Substances severely harmful to the aquatic environment. Must be marked if in non-bulk packages ≥ 882 lbs or bulk packages.
  • Elevated Temperature Materials: Liquids ≥ 212°F or solids ≥ 464°F during transport. Class 9, requires HOT placard on tank cars (not trucks).

Placards & Labels

When you need them, where they go, and what the rules say

🔴
Who Is Responsible for Placarding? The CARRIER (you/your company) is responsible for placarding the vehicle, not the shipper. However, the shipper must provide the correct placards or tell you which ones are needed. You cannot transport if proper placards are not available.

📐 Placard Specifications

  • Must be at least 10¾ inches (273mm) on each side — diamond shape
  • Must be readable from all 4 sides of the vehicle
  • Placed on front, rear, and both sides of vehicle
  • Must be at least 3 inches from other markings
  • Background must contrast with placard color
  • Must show the hazard class number in the bottom corner

📋 Table 1 vs. Table 2 — The Critical Distinction

Table Materials Quantity Threshold Rule
TABLE 1 Class 1.1, 1.2, 1.3 · Class 2.3 · Class 4.3 · Class 6.1 (PIH, Packing Group I) · Class 7 (radioactive label III) · Class Poison gas ANY amount Placard required regardless of quantity — even a single package
TABLE 2 All other hazmat classes (1.4, 1.5, 1.6, 2.1, 2.2, 3, 4.1, 4.2, 5.1, 5.2, 6.1, 6.2, 8, 9) 1,001 lbs or more (aggregate gross weight) Placard required only when total weight of ALL Table 2 materials ≥ 1,001 lbs
⚠️
Mixed Load Rule — Table 2 When you carry multiple different Table 2 materials, add ALL their weights together. If the combined total is 1,001 lbs or more, use the DANGEROUS placard — unless any single material is 5,000 lbs or more (then placard that specific material instead).

🚫 Placard Exceptions (When You Do NOT Need Placards)

  • Infectious substances (6.2) — no placard required
  • ORM-D materials
  • Limited quantities of certain materials (small packages)
  • Radioactive materials without Category III labels
  • Combustible liquids in non-bulk packaging — no placard unless combined weight ≥ 1,001 lbs
  • Class 1.4 Division S (1.4S) in small quantities

🏷️ Labels vs. Placards — Know the Difference

  • Labels go on individual packages — at least 3.9 inches (100mm) per side. Applied by the SHIPPER.
  • Placards go on the vehicle — at least 10¾ inches per side. Applied by the CARRIER.
  • Both must show the hazard class number in the bottom point of the diamond.
  • Labels are required when shipping "hazardous materials" — placards are required when a vehicle carries enough to reach thresholds.

Shipping Papers

The document that travels with every hazmat load

ℹ️
What Are Shipping Papers? Shipping papers (bills of lading, manifests, etc.) describe every hazardous material on board. They must be accurate, accessible, and legible at all times. Emergency responders use them to identify what's on your truck if you're in an accident.

📄 Required Basic Description — In This Order

The basic description must appear in this exact sequence:

  1. UN or NA Identification Number (e.g., UN1203)
  2. Proper Shipping Name (e.g., Gasoline)
  3. Hazard Class or Division (e.g., 3)
  4. Packing Group in Roman numerals (e.g., II) — if applicable

Example: UN1203, Gasoline, 3, II

Additional required info: Total quantity, number and type of packages, emergency response phone number.

⚠️
TRICK QUESTION: The Order Matters! The test will show you descriptions in wrong order. Always: ID Number FIRST, then Shipping Name, then Class, then Packing Group. Some materials don't have a packing group (Class 2 gases, explosives, radioactive) — that's OK, just leave it out.

📋 Additional Required Information on Shipping Papers

  • Emergency Response Telephone Number — must be monitored 24/7 by someone knowledgeable about the material (e.g., CHEMTREC 1-800-424-9300 is commonly used)
  • Shipper's certification — signed statement that the shipment is properly described, packaged, marked, and labeled
  • Reportable Quantity (RQ) — if the material has a reportable quantity, must appear before the proper shipping name or as "RQ" in the description
  • Poison Inhalation Hazard (PIH) — must be noted on shipping papers if applicable
  • Marine Pollutant — must be noted if applicable
  • Elevated temperature — noted with "HOT" if applicable

📍 Where to Keep Shipping Papers

  • While driving: within reach in the cab — in a pouch on the driver's door or on the seat
  • When out of the cab: on the driver's seat or in a pouch on the driver's door — clearly visible to anyone entering
  • Must be readily accessible at all times during transport

Loading & Unloading Rules

Segregation, forbidden combos, and what can't ride together

🔴
Absolutely Forbidden Combinations These materials can NEVER be loaded together in the same vehicle — no exceptions.

🚫 Hazmat + Food Rule

  • Poisons (Class 6.1 Packing Group I), poison gases (2.3), and infectious substances (6.2) cannot be loaded in the same vehicle with food, feed, or drugs for human or animal consumption
  • No separation distance can fix this — it is a forbidden combination
  • Exception: If materials are in fully enclosed containers that prevent contamination, some exceptions may apply — but this is rare in practice

📦 Loading Rules

  • No loading near open fires — must be at least 25 feet from open flames during loading/unloading of flammable materials
  • No smoking within 25 feet of any hazmat vehicle during loading/unloading
  • Engine off during fueling and while loading/unloading Class 1 explosives or flammable liquids (unless engine runs the pump)
  • Packages must be secured — blocking and bracing to prevent movement, shifting, or falling
  • Containers must be closed and secured before moving the vehicle
  • Never drop or throw packages of hazmat
  • Damaged packages must be reported and segregated

⚡ Attending the Vehicle During Loading/Unloading

  • Someone must be in attendance when loading/unloading Class 1 explosives, Class 2.3 poison gases, and certain other high-hazard materials
  • "Attended" means a person is within 50 feet, has the vehicle in sight, and is awake and alert — not sleeping in the cab
  • You may not leave a placarded vehicle unattended at a rest stop, fuel stop, or any public location — you must follow strict attendance rules
⚠️
Blocking & Bracing Requirements Hazmat packages must be secured so they cannot shift, tip, or fall during normal transport conditions. Use dunnage (wood, foam, etc.), bracing, or tie-downs as appropriate for the package type and hazard class. This is the CARRIER's responsibility.

Emergency Response

What to do when things go wrong on the road

🔴
Your #1 Priority in Any Emergency: KEEP PEOPLE SAFE Get away from the scene if you can't stop a leak. Warn others. Call for help. Do not attempt to handle hazmat spills unless you are trained and equipped. Your job is communication and evacuation, not cleanup.

📕 The Emergency Response Guidebook (ERG)

  • Published by PHMSA — a guide for first responders to identify hazardous materials and respond safely
  • Organized by UN/NA number and proper shipping name
  • Each material points to an orange guide page with response information
  • Drivers should know how to use the ERG but are NOT required to carry it (though it's strongly recommended)
  • The ERG is updated every 4 years
  • Look up: UN number → Guide page → Initial isolation distances and protective action distances

🚨 If You're in an Accident with Hazmat

  • Check for injuries — get medical help immediately
  • Keep people away from the area — set up a perimeter
  • Do NOT let anyone smoke or start fires near the area
  • Notify emergency services (911) immediately
  • Contact your dispatcher/company
  • Call the emergency response number on the shipping papers
  • Provide responders with the shipping papers, placards, and ERG guide number
  • Stay upwind and uphill from spills when possible

🔥 Fire in/near a Hazmat Vehicle

  • If cargo is on fire or you suspect a cargo fire: pull over, get out, move away, call 911
  • Do NOT fight cargo fires — hazmat requires specialized training and equipment
  • Tire fires: you may use dry chemical or CO₂ extinguisher on a tire fire carefully
  • Never use water on a 4.3 (Dangerous When Wet) material — it can cause violent reaction or toxic gas release
  • Never use water on a 5.1 oxidizer fire — can intensify the fire
  • Have a fire extinguisher rated 10 B:C or higher in the cab

💧 Spills & Leaks

  • Stop safely away from traffic
  • Do not attempt to stop a leak unless trained and equipped
  • Notify the National Response Center (NRC) at 1-800-424-8802 for reportable quantity spills
  • Notify your company immediately
  • Keep unauthorized people at least 300 feet away from unknown material spills
  • If you have a leak and can continue safely to a place to off-load without increasing the danger, do so — otherwise stop

Security Awareness & Plans

TSA requirements and your role in hazmat security

ℹ️
Post-9/11 Requirements All hazmat employees (including drivers) must receive security awareness training. Carriers that transport certain "select hazmat" must develop and follow a written security plan. This is required by 49 CFR Part 172, Subpart I.

📋 Who Needs a Security Plan?

  • Carriers transporting hazmat in quantities requiring placards
  • Carriers transporting SELECT AGENTS or TOXINS (any quantity)
  • Carriers transporting certain radioactive materials, infectious substances, or other high-hazard materials
  • The plan must be in writing and reviewed/updated at least annually

📝 What the Security Plan Must Include

  • Personnel security — background checks, authorization procedures
  • Unauthorized access prevention — how you protect cargo and vehicles
  • En-route security — procedures while transporting
  • Communication procedures — who to notify if something is wrong
  • Response procedures for security incidents
  • Training requirements

🚛 Driver Responsibilities — Security

  • Know your company's security plan and follow it
  • Be alert to suspicious behavior around your vehicle
  • Lock your cab when not in the vehicle
  • Keep hazmat shipment details confidential — do not discuss cargo with strangers
  • Report suspicious activity to law enforcement and your company
  • Never leave placarded vehicle unattended in a public place
  • Verify the identity of people who ask about your load

☢️ High-Risk Materials (Require Extra Security)

  • Explosives (Class 1) — especially 1.1, 1.2, 1.3
  • Poison inhalation hazard (PIH) materials — chlorine, anhydrous ammonia
  • Radioactive materials (Highway Route Controlled Quantities — HRCQ)
  • Infectious substances (6.2)
  • Materials meeting the definition of "select hazmat" — explosives above certain quantities, flammable liquids and gases above certain quantities
⚠️
TSA Background Check To obtain the Hazmat Endorsement, you must pass a TSA security threat assessment (fingerprinting, criminal background check, and immigration status verification). This is a federal requirement. Prior convictions for certain crimes permanently disqualify a driver from the HME.

Driving & Parking Rules

Special rules that apply when your truck carries hazmat

🚂 Railroad Crossings — CRITICAL TEST TOPIC

  • You MUST stop at ALL railroad crossings when carrying hazmat that requires placards — no exceptions
  • Stop between 15 and 50 feet from the nearest rail
  • Do not shift gears while crossing the tracks
  • Do not stop on the tracks — cross only when you are certain you can completely clear the tracks
  • Turn on 4-way flashers before stopping and while crossing
  • Look and listen in both directions before proceeding
🔴
TRICK QUESTION: Exempt Crossings A railroad crossing marked "EXEMPT" does NOT exempt hazmat vehicles. If you have a placarded load, you ALWAYS stop at all railroad crossings regardless of signs.

🅿️ Parking Rules for Hazmat Vehicles

  • Do NOT park within 5 feet of the traveled portion of a road if you can avoid it
  • Avoid parking in tunnels, on bridges, or in underpasses
  • Avoid populated areas, places where people gather, and areas near open flames or sparks
  • If you must stop on a roadway: turn on 4-way flashers and set out warning devices (triangles, flares — but avoid flares near flammable material)
  • Never park within 300 feet of an open fire

👁️ Attendance Requirements

  • A placarded hazmat vehicle must be attended at all times unless parked in a safe haven or attended facility
  • "Attended" = person is within 100 feet of the vehicle, can see it, and is awake
  • Explosives (1.1, 1.2, 1.3): Must ALWAYS be attended — even when stopped
  • Poison gas (2.3): Must always be attended
  • If unattended, vehicle must be parked in a locked facility or safe haven — locked, fenced area

🚇 Tunnels & Restricted Routes

  • Some tunnels, bridges, and urban routes are restricted for certain hazmat classes
  • You must obey all posted hazmat route restrictions
  • Class 1.1, 1.2, 1.3 explosives: Cannot use certain tunnels and bridges — check local routing rules
  • Radioactive materials (HRCQ): Require NRC-approved routes and advance notification in some states
  • Never take a hazmat load into a restricted area

⚠️ Other Driving Rules

  • No smoking within 25 feet of a tanker or vehicle carrying Class 1, 2.1, 3, 4, or 5 materials
  • Keep fire extinguisher charged and accessible at all times
  • Do not drive into a potentially dangerous area with hazmat
  • If you discover a leak enroute: safely exit traffic, warn others, call for help — do not continue with a leaking load unless it is safe to do so
  • Keep shipping papers accessible at all times while driving

Tanker-Specific Hazmat Rules

Cargo tanks, bulk packaging, and tanker endorsement requirements

ℹ️
Two Endorsements, Two Roles The Tank Vehicle (N) endorsement is required to drive tank trucks. The Hazmat (H) endorsement is required when those tanks carry hazardous materials. If you drive a placarded tanker, you need both — or the combined X endorsement.

📦 Bulk vs. Non-Bulk Packaging

  • Bulk packaging: A single container with a capacity of ≥119 gallons (liquid), ≥882 lbs (solid), or ≥882 lbs water capacity (gas) — cargo tanks, tank cars, intermediate bulk containers (IBCs)
  • Non-bulk packaging: Drums, boxes, cylinders, bags — smaller individual containers
  • Cargo tanks are the most common bulk packaging for highway transport

🔧 Cargo Tank Types

  • DOT MC-306/DOT406: Atmospheric pressure — flammable liquids (gasoline, diesel). Oval cross-section, rollover protection.
  • DOT MC-307/DOT407: Low pressure — chemicals, food-grade products. Circular cross-section, stronger than MC-306.
  • DOT MC-312/DOT412: High pressure corrosives — sulfuric acid, other corrosives. Smaller diameter, heavier construction.
  • DOT MC-331: High-pressure tank — compressed gases (propane, anhydrous ammonia). Round ends, pressure relief valves.
  • DOT MC-338: Cryogenic liquids — LNG, liquid nitrogen, liquid oxygen. Vacuum-jacketed insulated tank.

🔍 Pre-Trip Inspection — Tanker Hazmat

  • Inspect for leaks — body, fittings, manhole covers, valves, hoses
  • Check emergency discharge valves — must be closed and functioning
  • Verify dome covers/manhole covers are secure
  • Check vents and pressure relief devices — clear and operational
  • Verify ground bonding/grounding capability for flammable liquids
  • Check certification date on cargo tank — tanks must be inspected and tested on schedule (MC-306: annual internal inspection; MC-331: pressure test every 5 years)
  • Ensure correct placards are displayed

🌊 Liquid Surge & Stability

  • Liquid surge is the sloshing of liquid in a partially-filled tank — it can affect steering and braking
  • Baffled tanks: Have dividers that slow surge — less movement front/back but still side surge
  • Unbaffled (smooth bore) tanks: Full surge — most dangerous. Used for food and corrosives where cleaning is needed.
  • Surge effect is strongest when tank is 75–85% full — a partly filled tank is more dangerous than a full one
  • Speed up/slow down gently to manage surge forces
  • Take curves at reduced speed — outward surge can tip the tank
⚠️
Outage / Outage Requirements Liquid cargo tanks must have outage (space left at the top) to allow for thermal expansion of the liquid. The amount of outage depends on the material. Overfilling can cause pressure buildup or spills. The shipper is responsible for proper fill levels.

⚡ Grounding & Bonding for Flammable Liquids

  • When loading/unloading flammable liquids, the tank must be grounded to prevent static electricity buildup
  • Connect the ground wire before opening any valves or hatches
  • Do not disconnect the ground until after closing valves/hatches
  • Bond the tank to the receiving container to equalize electrical charge
