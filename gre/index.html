What each score looks like

Scoring guide, simplified›
ScoreWhat graders see
6A sharp, insightful position. Compelling, well-chosen examples. Smooth organization. Precise, varied language with almost no errors.
5A thoughtful position, developed with strong examples. Well organized, clear language, minor errors.
4A clear position with relevant reasons and examples. Adequately organized. Generally clear writing, some errors that don't block meaning.
3Some competence, but the position is vague or the support is thin. Weak organization or noticeable errors.
2Unclear position, little relevant support, or frequent errors that make it hard to follow.
1Little evidence of the ability to develop an argument. Often very short or off topic.

The jump from 3 to 4 is mostly about clarity and support: a plain position and real examples that are explained, not just named. That's the most reachable gain.

A five-paragraph plan that works

Template›

1. Introduction (3–4 sentences). Restate the issue in your own words. State your position in one sentence. Preview your two or three reasons.

2. Reason one. Topic sentence with the reason. A specific example from history, science, medicine, business or your own work. Two or three sentences explaining how the example proves the point.

3. Reason two. Same structure, with a different kind of example.

4. The other side. State the strongest objection fairly. Then explain why your position still holds, or where the claim is true and where it isn't.

5. Conclusion (2–3 sentences). Restate your position and why it matters. No new examples.

Clinical work is a great source of examples: patient safety, teamwork, protocols versus judgment, technology in care. Graders reward specific, real-feeling examples.
